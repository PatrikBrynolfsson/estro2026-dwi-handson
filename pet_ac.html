Tasks

Both photons have to get out.
  • FDG decays into two photons flying in opposite directions. The scanner counts the pair only if both reach the detectors.
  • Together they always cross the whole body along their line, wherever on the line they started. So what counts is the length of the line through the body.
  • Lines through the middle of the body cross the most tissue: through 30 cm of soft tissue, only about 1 pair in 18 gets out. Lines that graze the skin, or run through air-filled lung, lose little.
  • The scanner turns the CT (HU) into how strongly each voxel absorbs 511 keV photons, and multiplies each line back up.
Without AC there is no SUV. With AC, the CT is part of every PET value.

Why the tumour changes.
  • Every line through the tumour crosses the tumour itself: about 3 cm of soft tissue. When the moved CT puts air-filled lung there instead, those lines are corrected too little, and the tumour reads low.
  • At the liver dome the CT shows liver where the PET saw lung (or the reverse): a curved band that is too hot or too cold. Osman et al. saw such curved cold bands at the diaphragm in 84 % of PET/CT scans, mostly mild.
  • Erdi et al. corrected the same PET data with CTs from different breathing phases, as here: lesion SUVmax varied by up to 24 % (30 % over all phases).
Making it smaller.
  • Ask for shallow breathing during the CT, not a deep breath in (Goerres et al.: a deep breath in can badly degrade the PET).
  • An averaged CT (several breathing phases) gives smaller mismatch errors than a CT at the end of breathing out (Liu et al.).
  • Respiratory-gated PET with a matching 4D CT, where available.
  • And look at PET on CT at the liver dome every time, before you trust a number or a contour there.
The CT is in the PET. A breathing mismatch moves the SUV of a tumour near the diaphragm, with nothing changed in the patient.
